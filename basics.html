What are the basic skills of Relateful practice?

At its simplest, the basic skill of Relateful is tracking and expressing your present-moment experience while staying in relationship.

Most people do one of two things: they leave themselves and focus on the other person ("How are they feeling? What do they think of me?"), or they leave the relationship and go inward ("I'm having feelings, but I'm no longer connected to you.")

Relateful invites a third option: "Can I stay aware of what's happening in me and stay connected with you at the same time?" That sounds simple, but it's surprisingly difficult.

A beginner version might sound like: "As you speak, I notice myself relaxing." "I feel nervous saying this." "When you looked away, I felt a little disappointment." "I notice I'm trying to impress you right now." "I feel warmth toward you." "I'm confused."

Not analysis. Not storytelling. Not self-improvement. Just noticing and sharing what is happening now.

As people develop, a few supporting skills emerge:

  • Awareness — can I notice what's happening in my body, emotions, thoughts, impulses, and attention?
  • Disclosure — can I reveal some of that experience? Not all of it, just enough to become visible.
  • Ownership — can I say "I feel hurt" rather than "You hurt me"? The focus stays on your experience.
  • Curiosity — can I become interested in what's happening rather than immediately fixing, defending, or explaining it?
  • Staying — can I remain present when uncomfortable emotions arise? This is often the hardest skill.

Is staying present important in Relateful practice?

Yes. In many ways, staying present is the foundation that makes all the other Relateful skills possible. If awareness is noticing what's happening, and disclosure is sharing it, then presence is what allows you to notice it in the first place.

When people lose presence, they often drift into one of several places:

  • Story — "This always happens to me."
  • Analysis — "The reason you're doing that is..."
  • Future — "What does this mean for our friendship?"
  • Past — "This reminds me of what happened last year."
  • Protection — rehearsing what to say, managing impressions, defending against hurt.

None of those are wrong. But they move attention away from what's happening right now. Relateful often invites questions like "What's happening in your body as you say that?", "What's it like to hear me say this?", "What are you aware of right now?", or "What just shifted?" — questions that bring attention back to the present moment.

A useful distinction:

Presence sounds like "My chest feels tight," "I feel sadness," "I notice myself pulling away," "I want to move closer."

Meaning-making sounds like "This means you don't care about me," "This means we're incompatible," "This means I failed."

Meaning-making isn't forbidden — it's just that Relateful often asks people to spend more time in direct experience before drawing conclusions. You could even say one of the deepest Relateful skills is staying present long enough for reality to reveal itself, rather than rushing to explain it.

That doesn't mean abandoning discernment or boundaries. It means giving the present moment enough space that your understanding emerges from what is actually happening, not only from old patterns, fears, or hopes.

What does presence mean in Relateful?

"I am aware of where my attention is right now. Am I aware this is what I'm doing right now — am I aware that I'm choosing for my attention to be on this thing, as opposed to anywhere else? If I'm aware of that, I'm in presence."

— Annabeth

What do I do when I get triggered or activated during Relateful practice?

Getting triggered during Relateful is not a sign that you're doing it wrong. In fact, relational practice often brings people right up against the places where their nervous system learned about belonging, rejection, visibility, care, shame, or safety.

The important question isn't "How do I stop getting triggered?" but "What do I do once I notice I'm activated?"

First: Notice and name it

You can simply say: "I'm noticing a lot of activation." "My chest is getting tight." "I'm feeling flooded." "I'm having trouble staying present." You don't need to explain or justify it — naming activation is already regulating, because you're moving from being inside the experience to also observing it.

Second: Slow down

Many people speed up when activated. Try the opposite — take a breath, feel your feet, look around the room, notice the chair supporting you. The goal isn't to make the feeling disappear. It's to widen awareness enough that the feeling isn't the entire universe.

Third: Stay with the direct experience

Instead of "You don't care about me," try "When that happened, I felt a wave of hurt." Instead of "I'm being excluded," try "I'm noticing fear that I might be excluded." This keeps you connected to what is actually occurring in your experience.

Fourth: Ask for support if needed

Relateful is not a solo endurance test. You might say: "Can we slow down?" "I need some help navigating this." "Can you reflect back what you heard?" "Can we sit quietly for a moment?" A good relational field can help co-regulate without taking responsibility for your emotions.

Fifth: Know when you're beyond your window of tolerance

Sometimes you're not merely activated — you're flooded. Signs might include inability to think clearly, feeling trapped, overwhelming urgency, dissociation or numbness, inability to track the other person. At that point, the most skillful move may be: "I'm too activated to stay in connection right now." Taking a break is often more relational than pushing through.

A simple Relateful activation protocol:

  1. Notice: "I'm activated."
  2. Name sensations, emotions, and impulses.
  3. Slow down.
  4. Stay with your experience rather than conclusions.
  5. Ask for support if needed.
  6. Take a break if you're outside your window of tolerance.

The goal isn't to become untriggerable. The goal is to remain in enough relationship with yourself and the other person that the trigger becomes something you can explore, rather than something that takes over the entire interaction.

Direct experience and interpretation

One of the core invitations of Relateful is learning to distinguish between direct experience and interpretation.

Direct experience is what we're immediately aware of in the present moment — sensations in the body, emotions, desires, impulses, images, memories as they arise, whatever we're noticing right now. Examples: "My chest feels tight." "I notice sadness." "I want to move closer." "I'm nervous." "I feel warmth toward you."

Interpretation is the meaning we make about experience. Examples: "You're avoiding intimacy." "You don't trust me." "You're trying to control the conversation." "You see me as a threat."

Interpretation isn't inherently wrong — human beings naturally make meaning, and it can generate real insight, understanding, and connection. But it's different from direct experience, and direct experience is harder to dispute. If I say "my hands are shaking," there's little room for argument. If I say "you are afraid of intimacy," we've entered different territory. Neither statement is forbidden — the practice is learning to notice which territory we're occupying.

Storytelling, coaching, advice, teaching, analysis, and meaning-making can all be valuable forms of expression. Yet these can sometimes pull attention away from what's happening right now. A useful question: "What is happening in me that gives rise to this interpretation?"

Often beneath our stories we find something more immediate — fear, excitement, longing, confusion, tenderness, anger, uncertainty. Relateful doesn't ask us to stop interpreting. Rather, it invites us to remain connected to the living experience from which interpretation emerges.
